The gamma function has several important properties, including a recurrence relation:

\[
\Gamma(x+1) = x \Gamma(x)
\]

\[
\Gamma(n+1) = n!

Additionally, it can be expressed in terms of an infinite product for complex numbers with a positive real part:

\[

where \(\gamma\) is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

The gamma function also has representations involving logarithms and integrals, such as:

\[
\ln(\Gamma(x+1)) = \int_{0}^{1} \left( \frac{t^x - 1}{t-1} - x \right) \frac{dt}{\ln t}

For large values of \(x\), the gamma function can be approximated using asymptotic formulas. For example:

\[

with more precise forms given by series expansions or specific approximations like those of Lanczos, which provide better accuracy for a wider range of values.

\[

They have a contour integral representation given by:

\[

These polynomials are important in various scientific fields, including quantum mechanics and optics.

Q1. What is the Legendre polynomial? OK
The Legendre polynomial \(P_n(x)\) of degree \(n\) is a solution to the Legendre differential equation:

\[
(1 - x^2)y'' - 2xy' + n(n+1)y = 0.

These polynomials are orthogonal on the interval \([-1, 1]\) with respect to the weight function \(w(x) = 1\). They can be defined by the Rodrigues' formula:

\[
P_n(x) = \frac{1}{2^n n!} \frac{d^n}{dx^n} (x^2 - 1)^n.
\]

Additionally, they satisfy the recurrence relation:

\[
(n+1)P_{n+1}(x) = (2n+1)xP_n(x) -nP_{n-1}(x).
\]

The shifted Legendre polynomials are defined as \(P_n^*(x) = P_n(2x-1)\), which shift the domain from \([-1, 1]\) to \([0, 1]\).

This is known as a singular Sturm-Liouville equation. When \(\alpha = \beta = 0\), the Jacobi polynomial reduces to the Legendre polynomial, which are solutions of the Legendre differential equation.

where \(\Gamma\) is the gamma function and \(\delta_{mn}\) is the Kronecker delta, which equals 1 if \(m = n\) and 0 otherwise.
